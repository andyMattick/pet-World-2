## Enlightenment and Revolution (7.2)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Role: 🖨️ a printer of books and pamphlets, Paris, about 1770.

1. L1: A customer asks what Enlightenment writers trusted most to understand the world. What do you say?
   - **Human reason and evidence**
   - Ancient traditions passed down from the past
   - The commands of the king and his court
   - Old stories about luck and fate
2. L1: A customer wants the book that says people are born with natural rights to life, liberty, and property. Whose book is it?
   - **John Locke’s**
   - Isaac Newton’s
   - Edmund Burke’s
   - Napoleon Bonaparte’s
3. L2: A lawyer orders a book by Montesquieu. What idea is Montesquieu known for?
   - **Splitting government power among separate branches**
   - Giving one ruler full control so the laws are clear
   - Letting the Church write the kingdom’s laws
   - Ending the tax on bread and salt
4. L2: What does “sovereignty” mean?
   - **The supreme power to rule and make laws**
   - A trade route between two kingdoms
   - A tax collected on salt and grain
   - A church service held for the king and his family
5. L2: The king of France says he rules by “divine right.” What does he mean?
   - **His power comes from God, not from the people**
   - He was chosen by a vote of the citizens
   - He must obey a parliament of nobles
   - He was chosen by the nobles, who can remove him
6. L3: You print a book in which Locke argues (adapted): “If a government breaks the people’s trust, the people may set up a new one.” Which idea does this support?
   - **People may replace a government that abuses its power**
   - Kings receive their power directly from God
   - Subjects should obey their rulers in every case
   - Scientists should run the government instead of kings
7. L3: You print Rousseau’s famous line (adapted): “People are born free, but everywhere they are in chains.” What is he criticizing?
   - **Governments and customs that take away people’s freedom**
   - Children who do not obey their parents at home
   - Merchants who charge too much for printed books
   - Scientists who ignore new evidence about the natural world
8. L3: In 1792, Mary Wollstonecraft argued that women only seemed less capable because they were kept from education. How did she use Enlightenment ideas?
   - **She argued that reason and rights belong to women too**
   - She argued that kings should rule by divine right
   - She argued that tradition matters more than reason
   - She argued that schooling should be kept for landowners’ sons
9. L2: Voltaire is famous for defending which ideas?
   - **Free speech and religious tolerance**
   - The Church’s right to ban books
   - The divine right of the French king
   - Strict rules about what people may read
10. L3: At first, which group gained the most from Enlightenment ideas about rights?
   - **Mostly educated men who owned property**
   - Enslaved people in the colonies, who were freed at once
   - Women, who won the right to vote across Europe
   - Peasants, who were given land in each kingdom
11. L3: In 1790, Edmund Burke wrote about the French Revolution. What was his main warning?
   - **Tearing down old institutions at once could bring chaos and violence**
   - France should bring back divine-right kings in every European country
   - Reason was useless, so people should stop reading the new books
   - Revolutions make life better, so Britain should have one too
12. L3: Some people wanted revolution (fast change); others wanted evolution (slow change). Which is an example of evolution?
   - **A parliament slowly widening the vote over many years**
   - Crowds storming a royal prison and overthrowing the king
   - Rebels declaring a new country in a single summer
   - An army seizing the capital and crowning a new ruler
13. L1: Where do people in Paris often gather to talk about the new ideas you print?
   - **Salons and coffeehouses**
   - Royal prisons and forts
   - Army camps outside the city
   - Monasteries closed to visitors
14. L2: Looking back: how did the Scientific Revolution help lead to the Enlightenment?
   - **Science showed that reason and observation could explain nature, so thinkers used reason on society**
   - Science proved that the Sun moves around the Earth, so thinkers trusted ancient books even more
   - It ended trade between Europe and Asia
   - Science showed that nature cannot be understood, so thinkers stopped asking questions about society
15. L1: How does your printing press help spread the new ideas?
   - **Books and pamphlets reach many readers**
   - It prints money for the king’s army
   - It replaces schools and teachers
   - It keeps the new ideas secret from the public

Newer-type questions:

- Sort (L2): Sort each idea: is it an Enlightenment idea, or an old idea the Enlightenment challenged? Natural rights to life and liberty → **Enlightenment idea**; Kings rule by divine right → **Old idea it challenged**; Separate branches of government → **Enlightenment idea**; Freedom of religion → **Enlightenment idea**; The Church decides which books may be printed → **Old idea it challenged**; One ruler holds unlimited power → **Old idea it challenged**
- Part A (L3): Whose ideas most shaped the US Declaration’s claim that governments get their power from the consent of the governed? **John Locke** / Edmund Burke / Isaac Newton / Part B: Which of his ideas supports your answer? **People may replace a government that breaks their trust.** / Old traditions should be changed slowly, if at all. / The planets move by the law of gravity.
- Short answer (15+ words): Choose one Enlightenment thinker (Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire, or Wollstonecraft). Explain one of their ideas and how it challenged kings who claimed to rule by divine right. Rubric: Names a thinker and states one of their ideas accurately; Explains how the idea challenged rule by divine right (power comes from the people, limits on rulers, rights for everyone). Model: *John Locke said people are born with natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and that governments get their power from the people. That challenged divine right, because it meant a king’s power came from the people, not from God, and people could replace a ruler who abused it.*
