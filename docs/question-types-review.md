## English Unit 1: Nouns

### identifyNouns
- *Tap the words* (level 1): Tap every noun in the sentence.  
  Sentence: The **baker** sold warm **bread** to a hungry **boy.**
- *Tap the words* (level 2): Tap every noun in the sentence. Remember: ideas can be nouns too.  
  Sentence: Our **team** felt great **pride** after the **game** in **Denver.**
- *Short answer* (level –): A friend says nouns are just “things you can touch.” Explain why that is not the whole story, and give an example.  
  Rubric: Says nouns name people, places, things, and ideas / Gives a noun you cannot touch (an idea or feeling) / Uses the example correctly  
  Model: *Nouns name people, places, things, and ideas. Some nouns, like “courage” or “friendship,” name ideas you cannot touch, but they are still nouns.*

### singularPlural
- *Type the answer* (level 1): Type the plural of “box.”  
  Accepted: **boxes**
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort each plural by how it was made.  
  Added -s: **cats, trees** · Added -es: **dishes, foxes** · Changed y to -ies: **stories, berries**
- *Short answer* (level –): Explain when you add -es instead of -s to make a noun plural. Give two examples.  
  Rubric: Names an ending that takes -es (s, x, z, ch, or sh) / Gives two correct examples / Spells both plurals correctly  
  Model: *Add -es when a noun ends in s, x, z, ch, or sh, because you need an extra sound: bus becomes buses and lunch becomes lunches.*

### commonProper
- *Sort* (level 1): Sort the nouns.  
  Common noun: **city, holiday, river** · Proper noun: **Chicago, Thanksgiving, the Nile**
- *Tap the words* (level 2): Tap the proper nouns.  
  Sentence: Last **July,** my aunt drove to **Lake** **Erie** with her dog.
- *Short answer* (level –): Rewrite this sentence so the proper nouns are capitalized: “we visited mount rushmore in south dakota.” Then explain one proper-noun capital you added.  
  Rubric: Capitalizes We (the first word), Mount Rushmore, and South Dakota / Explains that proper nouns name a specific place / Leaves common words lowercase  
  Model: *We visited Mount Rushmore in South Dakota. “Mount Rushmore” gets capitals because it names one specific mountain carving, not mountains in general.*

### concreteAbstract
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort the nouns.  
  Concrete (you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell it): **thunder, pillow, perfume** · Abstract (an idea, feeling, or quality): **bravery, honesty, childhood**
- *Select all* (level 2): Select all the abstract nouns.  
  Select all: **kindness** · **fear** · **freedom** · pencil · drum · puppy
- *Short answer* (level –): Write one sentence that uses both a concrete noun and an abstract noun. Then label which is which.  
  Rubric: Writes a complete sentence / Includes a correct concrete noun and labels it / Includes a correct abstract noun and labels it  
  Model: *The trophy filled me with pride. “Trophy” is concrete because I can see and touch it; “pride” is abstract because it is a feeling.*

### fToVes
- *Type the answer* (level 1): Type the plural of “wolf.”  
  Accepted: **wolves**
- *Sort* (level 3): Which plurals change f or fe to -ves?  
  Changes to -ves: **leaf → leaves, knife → knives, shelf → shelves** · Just adds -s: **roof → roofs, chief → chiefs, cliff → cliffs**
- *Short answer* (level –): Your little cousin writes “three leafs fell.” Explain the mistake and how to fix it.  
  Rubric: Says the plural of leaf is leaves / Explains that some f words change f to v and add -es / Rewrites the sentence correctly  
  Model: *The plural of leaf is leaves. Some nouns that end in f change the f to v and add -es, so it should say “three leaves fell.”*

### enPlurals
- *Type the answer* (level 1): Type the plural of “child.”  
  Accepted: **children**
- *Select all* (level 2): Select all the nouns that form their plural with -en.  
  Select all: **ox** · **child** · dog · tooth · girl
- *Short answer* (level –): Most plurals end in -s. Explain what is different about “oxen” and “children,” and use one in a sentence.  
  Rubric: Says they add -en (or a special ending) instead of -s / Uses oxen or children correctly in a sentence  
  Model: *Oxen and children form the plural with an -en ending instead of -s. The children fed the oxen in the barn.*

### basePlurals
- *Select all* (level 2): Select all the nouns that stay the same for one and for more than one.  
  Select all: **sheep** · **deer** · **moose** · goat · horse · cow
- *Tap the words* (level 1): Tap the noun that is plural even though it doesn’t end in -s.  
  Sentence: Two **deer** drank from the stream near our cabin.
- *Short answer* (level –): How can you tell whether “sheep” means one animal or many? Use a sentence as an example.  
  Rubric: Explains that the rest of the sentence (number words, verb, “a/many”) shows it / Gives an example sentence that makes the number clear  
  Model: *Sheep is the same for one or many, so you look at other words. In “Three sheep are grazing,” the word “three” and the verb “are” show it is plural.*

### mutantPlurals
- *Type the answer* (level 1): Type the plural of “goose.”  
  Accepted: **geese**
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort the plurals.  
  Mutant plural (a vowel inside changes, no ending added): **mice, feet, women** · Another kind of plural: **buses, children, cacti**
- *Short answer* (level –): Why are words like “tooth/teeth” called mutant plurals? Give another example.  
  Rubric: Explains that the vowels inside the word change instead of adding an ending / Gives another correct example (man/men, foot/feet, mouse/mice, goose/geese, woman/women)  
  Model: *They are called mutant plurals because the inside of the word changes instead of adding -s. Another example is man and men.*

### foreignPlurals
- *Type the answer* (level 2): Type the plural of “cactus.” (Either correct plural counts.)  
  Accepted: **cacti**, **cactuses**
- *Sort* (level 3): Sort the borrowed nouns by how they become plural.  
  -us becomes -i: **fungus → fungi, stimulus → stimuli** · -is becomes -es: **crisis → crises, thesis → theses** · -on becomes -a: **phenomenon → phenomena, criterion → criteria**
- *Short answer* (level –): Some English nouns keep plurals from Latin or Greek. Pick one, show the singular and plural, and use the plural in a sentence.  
  Rubric: Gives a correct singular and plural pair / Uses the plural correctly in a sentence  
  Model: *Analysis becomes analyses. The scientists compared the analyses from both labs.*

### pluralReview
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort the plurals.  
  Regular (adds -s or -es): **glasses, pianos, churches** · Irregular: **women, knives, fish**
- *Two-part* (level 3): Part A: Which sentence uses the correct plural?  
  Part A: **The dentist cleaned both of my front teeth.** · The dentist cleaned both of my front tooths. · The dentist cleaned both of my front teeths.  
  Part B: Which rule explains your answer to Part A? **Some plurals change the vowels inside the word.** · Words ending in -th add -es. · Plural nouns never change their spelling.
- *Short answer* (level –): Write a sentence that uses three different kinds of irregular plurals (for example, -ves, -en, and a base plural). Underline or name them.  
  Rubric: Uses three irregular plurals correctly / Includes three different kinds of irregular plural / Writes a complete sentence  
  Model: *The children saw two wolves chasing a herd of deer. (children: -en plural; wolves: f to -ves; deer: base plural)*

## History Unit 1

### historyStories
- *Select all* (level 2): A museum asks you which sources could show what the battle was like for the people who were there. Select all that fit.  
  Select all: **A soldier’s diary from the battle** · **A letter a farmer wrote the week after** · **Arrowheads found in the field** · A movie made about the battle last year · A poem written 500 years later
- *Two-part* (level 3): Part A: Two histories of your city start in different years. What is the best explanation?  
  Part A: **Each writer chose a starting point that fit the story they were telling.** · One writer must have made a mistake about the facts. · The city was founded twice.  
  Part B: Which evidence would best support your answer to Part A? **One history is about the market; the other is about the first farms.** · Both histories are the same length. · Both writers lived in the city.
- *Short answer* (level –): Two towns tell different stories about the same flood. As the historian, what would you do with the two stories? Explain in 2–3 sentences.  
  Rubric: Compares the stories instead of picking one at random / Mentions checking evidence or each teller’s perspective / Explains what the comparison could show  
  Model: *I would compare the two stories and ask who told each one and what they could have seen. Then I would look for other evidence, like records of damage, to see which details hold up and why the towns remember it differently.*

### historyScale
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort each question by the scale it uses.  
  Close-up (one person, family, or town): **What did one baker sell each week?, How did one family survive the drought?, Who lived in this house?** · Big picture (many places or a long time): **How did trade routes connect three continents?, How did farming spread over 5,000 years?, Why did many cities grow in the same century?**
- *Put in order* (level 1): Put these scales in order from smallest to largest.  
  In order: **One family → A village → A whole region → The world**
- *Short answer* (level –): Why might a historian zoom in on one family’s letters after studying a big pattern across a whole region?  
  Rubric: Says the close-up shows how the pattern affected real people / Notes that one place can differ from the overall trend, or can test the pattern  
  Model: *The family’s letters show how the big change actually felt in people’s lives. They can also show that one place did not always follow the regional pattern, which tests whether the big claim is true everywhere.*

### historyFrames
- *Sort* (level 3): Which frame does each question use?  
  Communities: **How did neighbors share chores and celebrations?, What rules did the village follow?** · Networks: **How did news travel between ports?, Which roads connected the towns?** · Production and distribution: **Who made the cloth, and who sold it?, How was salt mined, and who sold it?**
- *Select all* (level 2): You are studying a big trade city. Select all the questions that use the networks frame.  
  Select all: **Which cities sent ships here?** · **How did ideas from far away arrive?** · Who made the pottery in the city? · How did families in one neighborhood help each other?
- *Short answer* (level –): Pick one frame (communities, networks, or production and distribution) and write a history question about your own town that uses it.  
  Rubric: Names a frame / Writes a real question about a town / The question fits the frame named  
  Model: *Production and distribution: Where does the food in our grocery store come from, and who makes money at each step?*

### historyMemory
- *Two-part* (level 3): Part A: A town holiday celebrates a famous victory, but letters from that time describe heavy losses. What does this show?  
  Part A: **Collective memory can leave out parts of what happened.** · The letters must be fake. · The holiday proves there were no losses.  
  Part B: Which question should a historian ask next? **Who wrote the letters, and who created the holiday?** · Which version is more fun to remember? · How many people go to the holiday now?
- *Select all* (level 2): Select all the questions that help you judge whether a remembered story is complete.  
  Select all: **Whose experiences are missing?** · **Who kept this story alive, and why?** · **What other evidence tells the same story?** · Is this the version I heard first? · Is the story exciting?
- *Short answer* (level –): Choose an event your family or school remembers. How might two people remember it differently? Explain why.  
  Rubric: Names an event / Describes two different memories of it / Explains a reason (perspective, role, what they saw)  
  Model: *At our school play, the actors remember being nervous backstage, while the audience remembers the funny ending. They remember different parts because they saw the event from different places.*

## Biology Unit 1

### cellsOrganisms
- *Put in order* (level 1): Put these in order from smallest to largest.  
  In order: **Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism**
- *Select all* (level 2): Select all the statements that are true of every living thing.

  Model: *An amoeba is alive because its one cell does every job of life: it takes in food, uses energy, gets rid of waste, and reproduces. It doesn’t need organs because the cell is the whole organism.*

### cellPartsU
- *Type the answer* (level 1): Which cell part controls the cell’s activities and holds its DNA? Type its name.  
  Accepted: **nucleus**, **the nucleus**
- *Sort* (level 2): Sort the cell parts.  
  Found in plant and animal cells: **nucleus, cell membrane, mitochondria, cytoplasm** · Found only in plant cells: **cell wall, chloroplast**
- *Short answer* (level –): Explain the difference between the cell membrane and the cell wall.  
  Rubric: Says the membrane controls what goes in and out / Says the cell wall is a stiff outer layer for support / Says only plant (and some other) cells have a cell wall  
  Model: *The cell membrane surrounds every cell and controls what moves in and out. A cell wall is a stiff layer outside the membrane that supports and protects plant cells; animal cells don’t have one.*

### asexualPlants

### sexualPlants

## Unit test paragraphs (all 7 units)

- **nouns**: Write a paragraph that teaches a younger student about irregular plural nouns. Include at least three kinds (for example f to -ves, -en, base plurals, mutant plurals, or foreign plurals), with an example of each. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: Explains what makes a plural irregular / Names at least three kinds with correct examples / Writes in complete, clear sentences a younger student could follow / Spells every plural correctly  
  Model: *Most nouns add -s to show more than one, but irregular plurals follow other patterns. Some words that end in f change to -ves, like leaf and leaves. A few add -en, like child and children. Some stay the same, like one deer and two deer. Others change the vowels inside, like mouse and mice. Words borrowed from other languages may keep their old plural, like cactus and cacti.*
- **verbs**: Write a paragraph about something you did last weekend. Use at least one verb in the past progressive, one in the present perfect, and one modal verb. Then list which verbs you used for each. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: Writes a clear paragraph about the weekend / Uses the past progressive correctly (was/were + -ing) / Uses the present perfect correctly (has/have + past participle) / Uses a modal verb correctly and labels all three  
  Model: *On Saturday I was helping my dad in the garden when it started to rain. I have planted tomatoes every spring, so I knew what to do. Next time we should check the weather first. (Past progressive: was helping. Present perfect: have planted. Modal: should.)*
- **history**: Pick an event your town, school, or family remembers. Write a paragraph explaining how a historian could tell the story of that event: the question they would ask, at least two sources they would use, one perspective that might be missing, and why comparing the sources matters. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: Names an event and the question a historian would ask / Names at least two sources and what each could show / Identifies a missing or different perspective / Explains why comparing evidence matters  
  Model: *Our school remembers the year the gym flooded. A historian could ask how the school recovered. They could use the principal’s letters to families and photos from the yearbook. The janitors’ point of view might be missing, even though they did much of the cleanup. Comparing these sources would show a fuller story than any one of them.*
- **history2**: Was farming a good change for early humans? Write a paragraph that makes a claim and supports it with at least two pieces of evidence. Then explain one way someone could disagree. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: States a clear claim / Gives at least two pieces of accurate evidence / Explains how the evidence supports the claim / Describes a counterargument  
  Model: *Farming helped humans as a group but was hard on many individuals. Farming produced a food surplus, so populations grew and towns formed. But farmers’ skeletons show more disease and shorter heights than foragers’, because their diets depended on a few crops. Someone could disagree by saying the surplus allowed jobs like pottery and writing, which made life better over time.*
- **history3**: Compare two early agrarian societies you studied (for example Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, or Shang China). Write a paragraph explaining one way they were alike and one way they were different, using evidence. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: Names two societies / Explains one similarity with evidence / Explains one difference with evidence / Connects the comparison to geography or resources  
  Model: *Mesopotamia and Egypt both grew beside rivers that flooded and left rich soil, which let farmers grow surplus grain and support cities. They were different in how they were ruled: Egypt was united under one pharaoh, while Mesopotamia was made up of separate city-states that often fought. Egypt’s long, protected river valley made unity easier.*
- **history4**: Choose a belief system or an empire you studied. Write a paragraph explaining how it spread or held power, using at least two specific examples. (at least 40 words)  
  Rubric: Names a belief system or empire / Explains how it spread or held power / Gives at least two specific, accurate examples / Explains how the examples connect to the claim  
  Model: *Buddhism spread from India across Asia mostly along trade routes. Merchants and monks carried its teachings on the Silk Roads, and travelers like Xuanzang brought texts back to China. As it spread, it changed to fit local cultures, which is why Buddhist temples in China look different from those in India.*
- **bio1**: Explain how your body gets energy from a meal. Follow the food from your mouth to your cells, naming at least four organs and what each does, and say where the energy is finally released. (at least 50 words)  
  Rubric: Names at least four digestive organs in order / Explains the job of each organ correctly / Explains that nutrients are absorbed into the blood and carried to cells / Says mitochondria release energy in the cells  
  Model: *Digestion starts in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva begins breaking down starch. The esophagus moves food to the stomach, which churns it with acid. In the small intestine, nutrients are absorbed through villi into the blood, and the large intestine absorbs water. The blood carries nutrients to every cell, where mitochondria release the energy in the food.*
