## The Holocaust (8.7)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

No role card here: she reads the sources as a historian. The lesson asks the grown-up PIN once (“Preview first”), and its practice is quiet: no celebration and no arcade minutes. Victims are named as people; there is no graphic detail.

1. L1: What was the Holocaust?
   - **The Nazi German state’s planned murder of about six million Jewish people**
   - A long battle between German and Soviet armies across Eastern Europe in the war
   - A famine in Eastern Europe during the war
   - A law that sent people to work in factories
2. L1: Historians use the word antisemitism. What does it mean?
   - **Hatred of or prejudice against Jewish people**
   - Opposition to war and armies
   - Support for one strong leader who rules without elections
   - Fear of people from other countries
3. L2: Germany’s Nuremberg Laws of 1935 (adapted) said Jewish people could no longer be citizens of Germany and banned marriages between Jews and other Germans. What step does this source show?
   - **Hatred became law, taking away Jewish people’s rights**
   - Germany protected the rights of minorities
   - Germany gave Jewish people new rights as full citizens of the state
   - Germany ended its army
4. L2: On November 9–10, 1938, Nazis attacked Jewish homes, shops, and synagogues across Germany. What is this event called?
   - **Kristallnacht, the “Night of Broken Glass”**
   - The Munich Agreement
   - The Beer Hall Putsch, Hitler’s failed Munich revolt
   - The Night of the Long Knives
5. L2: Besides Jewish people, which groups did the Nazis also persecute and murder?
   - **Roma people, disabled people, and political opponents**
   - Citizens of Germany’s allies, Italy and Japan, living in Berlin
   - Factory owners who made weapons for the army
   - Soldiers in the German army
6. L1: Anne Frank was a Jewish girl who hid with her family in Amsterdam for two years and kept a diary. What happened to her?
   - **She was found, deported, and died in the Bergen-Belsen camp in 1945**
   - She escaped by ship to the United States and later became a famous writer there
   - She stayed hidden safely in Amsterdam until the war ended
   - She was taken to Switzerland by the Red Cross
7. L2: In 1938, delegates from 32 countries met at Évian, France, to discuss Jewish refugees trying to leave Germany. What happened?
   - **Most countries refused to take in many more refugees**
   - The countries declared war on Germany
   - Germany agreed to stop its attacks on Jewish people right away
   - The countries opened their borders to the refugees
8. L3: In 1939, the ship St. Louis carried more than 900 Jewish refugees from Germany. Cuba, the United States, and Canada turned it away, and it sailed back to Europe. What does this show about the world’s response?
   - **Many countries closed their doors to people fleeing the Nazis**
   - Refugees were welcomed wherever they went
   - Germany refused to let the ship leave its port in Hamburg harbor
   - The ship was carrying soldiers, not refugees
9. L3: Manuel Quezon, president of the Philippines (then under U.S. control), helped about 1,300 Jewish refugees settle in Manila between 1937 and 1941. What does his story show?
   - **Some leaders chose to help, even when most of the world did little**
   - The Philippines was part of Nazi Germany
   - Refugees could travel freely to any country they wanted at the time
   - The United States stopped the plan
10. L3: In 1940, Chiune Sugihara, a Japanese diplomat in Lithuania, wrote more than 2,000 travel visas for Jewish refugees against his government’s orders. Historians call people like him...
   - **Rescuers**
   - Bystanders
   - Perpetrators
   - Collaborators
11. L2: Historians use the word “bystander” for someone who...
   - **Saw what was happening but did not act to help**
   - Hid Jewish families in their home
   - Fought against Germany as a soldier in the Allied armies
   - Wrote visas for refugees
12. L3: In December 1942, the Allies publicly declared (adapted) that Germany was carrying out its plan to destroy the Jewish people of Europe, and promised to punish those responsible. Why is this source important to historians?
   - **It shows Allied leaders knew about the mass murder during the war**
   - It proves that the Allies first learned of the killings in May 1945
   - It was written by Nazi leaders
   - It ended the Holocaust right away
13. L3: After the war, Nazi leaders were put on trial at Nuremberg (1945–1946). What idea did these trials help establish?
   - **Leaders can be held responsible for crimes against humanity**
   - Following orders from a leader is an excuse for committing any crime
   - Soldiers are the ones who can be tried for war crimes
   - A country’s leaders cannot be judged by other nations
14. L3: Historians found that many ordinary people, such as police officers, clerks, and railway workers, took part in carrying out the Holocaust. Why does this matter?
   - **Genocide needs many people’s help, so each person’s choices matter**
   - It shows Hitler carried out the killing by himself
   - It shows the killing took place on battlefields between two armies at war
   - It shows ordinary people knew nothing about it
15. L2: The word “genocide” was created in 1944 by Raphael Lemkin, a Polish Jewish lawyer. What does it mean?
   - **Acts meant to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group**
   - A war between two countries that ends with one side surrendering to the other
   - A law that takes away the right to vote
   - Moving people to new farms

Chiune Sugihara writes visas in Lithuania → **Helped them escape**; Most countries at the Évian Conference → **Turned them away**; Manuel Quezon opens Manila to refugees → **Helped them escape**; The countries that refused the ship St. Louis → **Turned them away**

- Short answer: Choose one person or country from this lesson. Explain how they responded to Nazi persecution, and what we can learn from that choice. Rubric: Names a person or country and describes its response accurately; Explains a lesson about responsibility (helping, speaking up, or the cost of doing nothing). Model: *Chiune Sugihara, a Japanese diplomat, wrote visas for Jewish refugees even though his government told him not to. His choice helped thousands of people escape and shows that one person who refuses to look away can save lives.*

## Causes of the First World War (8.2)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Lesson text: World War I (1914–1918) grew from four long-term causes, remembered as MAIN: militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism. The spark was the killing of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in June 1914, and the alliances pulled the great powers in.

1. L1: A reader asks whose killing on June 28, 1914, set off the war. What do you say?
   - **Archduke Franz Ferdinand**
   - Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany
   - Tsar Nicholas II of Russia
   - King Peter I of Serbia
2. L1: Historians remember four long-term causes of the war with the letters MAIN. What do they stand for?
   - **Militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism**
   - Monarchy, armies, industry, navies
   - Migration, agriculture, inflation, and newspapers
   - Money, artillery, invasions, neutrality
3. L1: In 1914, Britain, France, and Russia were on one side. What was the other side, led by Germany and Austria-Hungary, called?
   - **The Central Powers**
   - The Axis Powers
   - The Warsaw Pact
   - The Triple Entente
4. L2: The young man who fired the shots belonged to a group that wanted Bosnia’s South Slavs to join Serbia. Which cause of war does this show?
   - **Nationalism**
   - Militarism
   - Imperialism
   - Industrialization
5. L2: Germany and Britain are racing to build bigger battleships, and generals have detailed plans ready for war. Which cause of war is this?
   - **Militarism**
   - Nationalism
   - Alliances
   - Appeasement
6. L2: Britain, France, and Germany have been competing for colonies in Africa and Asia. Which cause of war is this?
   - **Imperialism**
   - Militarism
   - Isolationism
   - Communism
7. L2: After Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia, Russia, Germany, France, and Britain join within days. Why?
   - **Alliances and war plans pulled each great power in**
   - Serbia attacked each of them in the same week
   - The League of Nations ordered them to join
   - They had agreed to divide up Serbia’s colonies among themselves
8. L3: Britain’s foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, said in August 1914 (adapted): “The lamps are going out all over Europe; we shall not see them lit again in our lifetime.” What did he mean?
   - **He feared a long war that would change Europe for good**
   - He was warning about a shortage of oil for the city’s street lamps
   - He expected a short war that would be over by Christmas
   - He was celebrating that Britain would win quickly
9. L3: In July 1914, Germany told Austria-Hungary it would back it no matter what. Historians call this the “blank check.” Why did it matter?
   - **It made Austria-Hungary bolder in its threats to Serbia**
   - It meant Britain would stay out of the war
   - It ended the alliance between Germany and Austria-Hungary
   - It forced Serbia to give up its army
10. L3: Austria-Hungary’s ultimatum to Serbia (July 1914, adapted) demanded that Austro-Hungarian officials help run the investigation inside Serbia. Serbia accepted most demands but refused this one. Why?
   - **It would let a foreign power act inside Serbia and weaken its independence**
   - Serbia had no police force that could run an investigation
   - The demand was written in German, a language Serbian officials were unable to read
   - Serbia had already joined the Central Powers
11. L3: Why do historians call World War I a global war, even though much of the fighting was in Europe?
   - **Empires brought in soldiers, workers, and fighting from around the world**
   - Most of the battles were fought in Africa and South America
   - It began with a Japanese attack in the Pacific and then spread west into Europe
   - It was the first war fought only at sea
12. L2: Before 1914, Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire ruled many different peoples. Why did this make war more likely?
   - **Groups inside them wanted nations of their own, causing unrest**
   - They had just defeated Britain and France in a long and costly war
   - Their many peoples spoke a single shared language
   - They had given up their armies after 1900
13. L1: In what year did World War I begin?
   - **1914**
   - 1939
   - 1917
   - 1905
14. L3: A historian studies World War I using the networks frame. Which question fits that frame?
   - **How did alliances and empires link countries and spread the war?**
   - Who made the weapons, and who profited from selling them to armies?
   - How did families in one French village get through the war years?
   - In what year did the fighting finally stop?
15. L3: One historian argues Germany was most to blame for the war. Another argues the great powers shared the blame. What should a careful historian do?
   - **Weigh the evidence about each country’s choices before deciding**
   - Pick the view of the more famous historian
   - Accept whichever of the two views was published first in a book
   - Decide that nobody’s choices caused the war

Newer-type questions:

- Sort: Was each one a long-term cause of World War I or the short-term trigger? Britain and Germany race to build battleships → **Long-term cause**; European powers compete for colonies → **Long-term cause**; Nationalism grows in the Balkans → **Long-term cause**; Franz Ferdinand is shot in Sarajevo → **Short-term trigger**
- Order: Put the events of summer 1914 in order. **Franz Ferdinand is shot in Sarajevo (June 28) → Austria-Hungary sends Serbia an ultimatum (July 23) → Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia (July 28) → Germany invades Belgium, and Britain declares war (August 4)**
- Short answer: Explain how one assassination in Sarajevo turned into a war among many countries. Rubric: Names the assassination of Franz Ferdinand as the spark; Explains how alliances (or long-term causes like militarism and nationalism) pulled other countries in. Model: *When a Bosnian Serb nationalist killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia. Because of alliances, Russia backed Serbia, Germany backed Austria-Hungary, and France and Britain joined Russia, so a local crisis became a world war.*

## The First Total War (8.3)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Lesson text: In a total war, a country puts its whole society to work for the war: factories, farms, women workers, colonies, and propaganda. World War I was fought in trenches, at sea, and across empires, with new weapons like machine guns, poison gas, tanks, and airplanes.

1. L1: Many of your patients come from long ditches the armies dug along the front line. What were these called?
   - **Trenches**
   - Bunkers
   - Canals
   - Tunnels
2. L1: A visitor asks what a “total war” is. What do you say?
   - **A war where a country uses its whole society and economy to fight**
   - A war fought by professional soldiers while life at home stays the same
   - A war that ends in a single big battle
   - A war fought between just two countries on one small battlefield
3. L2: At home, many British women took jobs making shells for the army. Why?
   - **Men had left for the front, and the war needed huge amounts of weapons**
   - Women were required by law to join the army
   - Factories had closed and needed new owners
   - The government had passed a law banning men from working in any factory
4. L2: Which new weapons made World War I so deadly?
   - **Machine guns, poison gas, and heavy artillery**
   - Atomic bombs, jet fighters, and guided rockets
   - Muskets loaded one shot at a time
   - Radar and computers
5. L2: Why did the Western Front barely move for years?
   - **Trenches, barbed wire, and machine guns made attacks very costly**
   - Both armies signed an agreement to stop fighting until the year 1918
   - Soldiers refused to leave their trenches
   - Snow covered the battlefield year-round
6. L1: Which country joined the Allies in 1917 after German submarines attacked its ships?
   - **The United States**
   - Japan
   - Italy
   - The Ottoman Empire
7. L2: Japan joined the Allies in 1914. What did it gain from the war?
   - **German colonies and bases in China and the Pacific**
   - Control of the Suez Canal
   - Land in the American Southwest that Germany had promised it
   - Most of India from Britain
8. L3: An Indian soldier in France wrote home in 1915 (adapted from letters collected by British army censors): “This is not war. It is the ending of the world.” What does this source show?
   - **Soldiers from the colonies fought in Europe and were shocked by the war**
   - Indian soldiers stayed in India for the whole war
   - The war in France was short and easy for soldiers who came from the colonies
   - Indian soldiers were not allowed to write home
9. L3: Why must historians be careful when they use letters like that one?
   - **Army censors read them, so soldiers may not have written everything**
   - They were written long after the war ended
   - They were written by newspaper reporters who pretended to be soldiers
   - They were written in code that no one has broken
10. L3: Helen Fairchild, an American nurse, worked at a hospital close to the front in 1917 and died there in early 1918 after falling ill. What does her story show about total war?
   - **Women who were not soldiers also served near the front and faced danger**
   - Nurses were kept in safe hospitals far away in their own home countries
   - Women were kept out of the war
   - The war was mostly over by 1917
11. L3: Rosa Luxemburg, a socialist in Germany, spoke out against the war and was sent to prison. What does her story show?
   - **Some people opposed the war, and governments punished them**
   - Germans agreed that the war was a good idea
   - Germany let critics protest freely and printed their speeches in newspapers
   - Socialists were in charge of the German army
12. L3: A British poster shows an officer pointing at the reader with the words “Join your country’s army!” Why did governments make posters like this?
   - **To build support and recruit soldiers for the war**
   - To advertise new jobs in other countries
   - To warn young men to stay home and avoid joining the army
   - To sell tickets to war films
13. L2: How was the Eastern Front different from the Western Front?
   - **It moved more, with armies advancing and retreating over long distances**
   - It was fought mostly by ships in the North Sea
   - It stayed locked in the same trenches across northern France from 1914 to 1918
   - It was fought between Britain and the United States
14. L2: How did the fighting in World War I stop in November 1918?
   - **Germany, worn out and short of food, signed an armistice**
   - Germany captured Paris and won the war
   - The Treaty of Versailles was signed on the very same day as the ceasefire
   - Russia defeated Germany on the Eastern Front
15. L1: Which Allied country fought Austria-Hungary high in the mountains of the Alps?
   - **Italy**
   - Spain
   - Japan
   - Sweden

Newer-type questions:

- Select all: Select every way World War I was a total war. **Factories switched to making weapons**; **Governments used propaganda posters**; **Women took jobs left by soldiers**; Life at home stayed the same as before; Countries kept their economies the way they were
- Part A: Why did so many soldiers die attacking across no man’s land? **Machine guns and barbed wire stopped attackers in the open** (other choices: The fighting took place on ships at sea; Soldiers had been sent into battle without rifles) / Part B: What did armies try by the end of the war to break the stalemate? **New tools like tanks and airplanes** (other choices: Going back to knights in armor; Moving the whole war to Asia)
- Short answer: Explain one way World War I affected people at home, not just soldiers. Rubric: Names an accurate home-front effect (factory work, women’s jobs, food shortages, propaganda, loss of loved ones); Explains why it happened or how it changed daily life. Model: *Because so many men went to fight, women in Britain took jobs in factories making shells. This changed daily life and showed that the whole country, not just the army, was working for the war.*

## A World Remade (8.4)  (14 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Lesson text: The Treaty of Versailles (1919) blamed Germany, took its land and colonies, made it pay reparations, and set up the League of Nations. Old empires broke apart, Britain and France took over Ottoman Arab lands, and revolutions remade Russia and Mexico.

1. L1: Readers ask which treaty with Germany was signed at the peace conference in 1919. What do you say?
   - **The Treaty of Versailles**
   - The Treaty of Westphalia
   - The Munich Agreement
   - The Treaty of Tordesillas
2. L1: Which new organization did the peace plan create to settle arguments between nations?
   - **The League of Nations**
   - The United Nations
   - NATO
   - The Congress of Vienna
3. L2: Article 231 of the treaty (adapted) said Germany and its allies were responsible for the loss and damage of the war. What did this lead to?
   - **Germany had to pay large reparations and felt humiliated**
   - Germany received money from France
   - Germany joined the League of Nations right away
   - Germany was given new colonies in Africa to make up for its losses
4. L2: U.S. President Woodrow Wilson proposed the Fourteen Points. Which idea was one of them?
   - **Self-determination: peoples choosing their own governments**
   - Splitting Germany into four zones run by the winning countries
   - Building a wall across Berlin
   - Letting each winner keep the land it had taken
5. L3: Wilson spoke of self-determination, yet most colonies in Africa and Asia did not get independence in 1919. How did many colonized people react?
   - **They felt let down, and independence movements grew stronger**
   - They were satisfied with the treaty and dropped their demands for freedom
   - They moved to Europe to work for the League
   - They declared war on the United States
6. L2: During the war, Britain and France secretly agreed to divide the Ottoman Empire’s Arab lands between them. What was this deal called?
   - **The Sykes-Picot Agreement**
   - The Balfour Declaration
   - The Treaty of Versailles
   - The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk
7. L3: Britain had also told Arab leaders they would get independence if they revolted against the Ottomans. Why did many Arabs feel betrayed after the war?
   - **Their lands became British and French mandates instead**
   - The Ottoman Empire won the war in the Middle East and kept its lands
   - Britain gave Arabia to Germany
   - The League made them join the Soviet Union
8. L3: Britain’s Balfour Declaration (1917, adapted) supported “a national home for the Jewish people” in Palestine while protecting the rights of the people already living there. Why did it lead to conflict later?
   - **Arab and Jewish communities both had hopes for the same land**
   - It handed control of Palestine to France as part of the Sykes-Picot deal
   - It was cancelled the next week
   - It moved the Arab people of Palestine to Egypt
9. L2: In 1917, Russian workers and soldiers were tired of war and hunger. Which slogan did Lenin’s Bolsheviks use to win their support?
   - **Peace, land, and bread**
   - Liberty, equality, fraternity
   - Blood and iron
   - No taxation without representation
10. L3: After the Bolsheviks took power, what did Russia do?
   - **It left the war and set up a communist state, later the Soviet Union**
   - It joined Germany and Austria-Hungary in a brand-new attack on France and Britain
   - It elected a new tsar
   - It became a colony of Britain
11. L2: The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) was fought partly over land. What did leaders like Emiliano Zapata demand?
   - **Land returned to poor farmers and villages**
   - A new king for Mexico
   - Rule by Spain again
   - Bigger estates for the wealthy landowners and their families
12. L3: How were the Mexican and Russian Revolutions alike?
   - **Both grew from poor people’s anger over land and power**
   - Both brought back a monarchy
   - Both happened because World War II had left the countries in ruins
   - Both turned their countries into colonies
13. L3: Some writers after the war were called a “lost generation.” Why?
   - **Many young people felt the war had destroyed their hopes and beliefs**
   - They had moved to colonies in Asia and Africa
   - They refused to write about the war
   - They were too young to remember any of the fighting from the war years
14. L2: Which empires broke apart because of World War I?
   - **Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and the Russian Empire**
   - The British Empire, the French Empire, and the Empire of Japan
   - The Mughal and Ming Empires
   - The Roman and Han Empires

Newer-type questions:

- Sort: Did each change come from the Paris peace treaties or from a revolution? Germany loses land and pays reparations → **The peace treaties (1919)**; The League of Nations is created → **The peace treaties (1919)**; The Bolsheviks take power in Russia → **A revolution**; Zapata’s fighters demand land for villages in Mexico → **A revolution**
- Part A: Which claim about the Treaty of Versailles is best supported? **It punished Germany harshly and left many Germans angry** (other choices: It treated Germany as one of the winners; It gave Germany new colonies) / Part B: Which evidence supports your answer? **Germany had to accept blame, give up land, and pay reparations.** (other choices: Germany led the League of Nations from its first meeting.; Germany received Britain’s colonies in Africa.)
- Short answer: Pick one group (Germans, Arabs in the former Ottoman lands, colonized peoples, Russians, or Mexican farmers). Explain how the end of World War I or a revolution changed life for them. Rubric: Names a group and an accurate change (treaty, mandate, revolution, or land reform); Explains how the change affected that group’s lives or hopes. Model: *Many Arabs had fought the Ottomans because Britain promised them independence. After the war, Britain and France took over their lands as mandates instead, so Arab leaders felt betrayed and kept pushing for self-rule.*

## Interwar Ideologies (8.5)  (14 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Lesson text: The Great Depression (from 1929) left millions out of work. In some countries people turned to fascism: one all-powerful leader, extreme nationalism, no rival parties, and violence against opponents. Mussolini in Italy and Hitler in Germany took power this way, while the League of Nations tried to keep the peace.

1. L1: In 1929, the U.S. stock market crashed. Listeners ask what worldwide crisis followed. What do you say?
   - **The Great Depression**
   - The Cold War
   - The Industrial Revolution
   - The Dust Bowl
2. L1: Who led the Fascist Party in Italy and took power in 1922?
   - **Benito Mussolini**
   - Adolf Hitler
   - Joseph Stalin
   - Francisco Franco
3. L2: Which best describes fascism?
   - **One all-powerful leader, extreme nationalism, and no rival parties**
   - Free elections with many parties and a free press
   - Workers sharing ownership of farms and factories
   - Rule by a king or queen who shares power with an elected parliament
4. L2: Why did many Germans support the Nazis in the early 1930s?
   - **The Depression left millions jobless, and the Nazis promised jobs and national pride**
   - The Nazis promised to pay even more reparations to France and Britain for the next fifty years
   - Germany had just won World War I
   - The Nazis promised to give up Germany’s army
5. L2: In January 1933, how did Hitler become chancellor of Germany?
   - **He was appointed after the Nazis became the largest party in elections**
   - He led a large private army that marched in and captured Berlin by force
   - He was chosen by the League of Nations
   - He was born into Germany’s royal family
6. L3: Once in power, how did the Nazis turn Germany into a dictatorship?
   - **They banned other parties, took over the press, and used terror against opponents**
   - They let independent courts and a free press check and overrule Hitler’s decisions
   - They held free elections each year
   - They shared power with the Communists
7. L3: Mussolini wrote (paraphrased, 1932) that the state is everything and a person matters as part of the state. Which feature of fascism does this show?
   - **The state controls people’s lives instead of protecting their rights**
   - People can freely criticize their government
   - Power is shared among many parties chosen in free and fair elections
   - Each region rules itself
8. L2: Between the wars, which idea did the League of Nations stand for?
   - **Internationalism: nations working together to keep peace**
   - Isolationism: staying out of world affairs
   - Imperialism: building bigger empires by conquest of others
   - Fascism: one leader ruling by force
9. L3: Why did the League of Nations fail to stop aggression in the 1930s?
   - **It had no army, and the United States never joined**
   - It had the strongest army in the world
   - It was run by Germany and Italy, who used it to plan their attacks
   - It had broken up before 1930
10. L3: From 1939, Thailand’s prime minister, Plaek Phibunsongkhram, issued rules on how people should dress and speak and promoted a national dish, pad thai. Which interwar idea does this show?
   - **A nationalist leader using culture to unite and control people**
   - A king giving his power to elected leaders
   - A colony in Southeast Asia winning its freedom from British and French rule
   - Communism taking over farms
11. L2: From 1911 to 1949, which two groups fought for control of China?
   - **The Nationalists (Guomindang) and the Communists**
   - The emperors of the Ming dynasty and the Qing dynasty
   - The British and the French
   - Japan and Korea
12. L3: Why did Mussolini’s Italy and Hitler’s Germany become allies in the 1930s?
   - **Both were fascist states that wanted to expand by force**
   - Both had lost World War I and wanted to stay out of new wars
   - Both were communist states
   - Both were ruled by the League of Nations
13. L1: What is propaganda?
   - **Information spread to push people toward one view**
   - A secret police force
   - A kind of election
   - A trade agreement signed between two or more countries
14. L3: A historian says fascism grew out of a crisis. Which context best supports this?
   - **War losses, anger over the peace, and the Depression made people want strong leaders**
   - Europe was peaceful and rich, and nearly everyone had a good, steady job in the 1930s
   - Most countries had just become democracies with few problems
   - The League of Nations had banned elections

Newer-type questions:

- Sort: Sort each feature: does it belong to fascism or to democracy? One leader with total power → **Fascism**; Free elections with several parties → **Democracy**; Opposition parties banned → **Fascism**; A free press that can criticize leaders → **Democracy**
- Select all: Select every cause that helped fascists win support between the wars. **The Great Depression and lost jobs**; **Anger at the peace treaties after World War I**; **Fear of communism**; A long period of peace and wealth; Strong trust in the League of Nations
- Short answer: Explain why people in a crisis might support a leader who promises to fix everything, and one danger of doing that. Rubric: Explains a reason (no jobs, fear, anger, wanting order and pride); Names a danger (loss of rights, no elections, violence against opponents, war). Model: *During the Depression many Germans had no jobs and felt humiliated by the peace treaty, so a leader who promised work and pride sounded good. The danger was that Hitler then banned other parties and took away people’s rights.*

## The Second World War (8.6)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

## The Cold War (8.8)  (15 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

Lesson text: After 1945, the United States (democracy and capitalism) and the Soviet Union (communism and one-party rule) became rival superpowers. They never fought each other directly, but they raced to build nuclear weapons and reach space and backed opposite sides in wars like Korea and Vietnam. The Cold War ended when the Soviet Union broke apart in 1991.

1. L1: A visitor asks which two superpowers led the two sides of the Cold War. What do you say?
   - **The United States and the Soviet Union**
   - The British Empire and the French Empire
   - Germany and Japan
   - China and India
2. L1: Why was it called a “cold” war?
   - **The two superpowers never fought each other directly**

## Decolonization (8.9)  (14 multiple choice + 3 newer-type)

   - Empires taking over new colonies
