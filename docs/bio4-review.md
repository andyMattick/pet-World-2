## Chromosomes and the cell cycle (Quiz 1)

What chromosomes are, how many a cell has, and how one cell copies its DNA and splits into two identical cells by mitosis.

**Why it matters: The threads inside a salamander.** In the late 1870s, a German doctor named Walther Flemming stained cells from salamander larvae with dye and looked at them under a microscope. Inside the nucleus he saw thread-like shapes that thickened, lined up, and split apart as the cell divided in two. In 1882 he named the process mitosis, from the Greek word for thread. He didn’t know it yet, but those threads were chromosomes, carrying the instructions for building the salamander.

### Chromosome structure and numbers (`chromosomes4`)

Body cells have two sets (diploid); egg and sperm have one set (haploid).

Why care? Doctors count chromosomes to find conditions like Down syndrome, which comes from a third copy of chromosome 21.

### The cell cycle and mitosis (`mitosis4`)

Lesson: The cell cycle is a cell’s life: in interphase it grows and copies its DNA; in mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) the copies are pulled apart, and each separated chromatid counts as its own chromosome; then cytokinesis splits the cell in two. Mitosis makes two identical cells with the same number of chromosomes as the parent. Living things use it to grow, heal, and replace cells.

Why care? Every scraped knee heals by mitosis, and cancer is what happens when cells keep dividing when they should stop.

## Meiosis and types of reproduction (Quiz 2)

How meiosis makes egg and sperm cells with half the chromosomes, and how asexual and sexual reproduction compare.

**Why it matters: Why mules almost never have babies.** A mule is the offspring of a horse and a donkey. Horses have 64 chromosomes and donkeys have 62, so a mule gets 32 + 31 = 63. Mules are strong and healthy, but when a mule’s cells try to make egg or sperm cells by meiosis, the chromosomes can’t all line up in matching pairs. That is why mules are almost always unable to have offspring of their own.

### Meiosis (`meiosis4`)

Lesson: Meiosis makes sex cells (eggs and sperm). The DNA is copied once, then the cell divides twice, making four cells that each have half the chromosomes: one from every pair. Crossing over and the way the pairs line up make every sex cell different. Fertilization brings the full number back.

Why care? Meiosis is why brothers and sisters with the same parents can look so different.

## Fertilization, development, and life cycles (Quiz 3)

How a fertilized egg becomes an embryo of specialized cells, and how butterflies, grasshoppers, frogs, and plants grow through their life cycles.

**Why it matters: The great-grandchildren come home.** Every fall, monarch butterflies from the eastern United States and Canada fly as far as 3,000 miles to the same mountain forests in central Mexico. Summer monarchs live only about two to six weeks, but this special fall generation can live about eight months. In spring they head north, lay eggs on milkweed, and die. It takes several generations to get back to Canada, so the butterflies that return to Mexico the next fall have never been there before.

## Unit test paragraph

You are the head tour guide at the Pet World Butterfly House. Write a paragraph that follows the monarchs from one generation to the next. Use meiosis, fertilization, mitosis, and the four stages of complete metamorphosis, and say what each one does.

- Rubric: Explains that meiosis makes egg and sperm cells with half the chromosomes; Explains that fertilization makes a zygote with the full number, which grows by mitosis; Names the four stages (egg, larva, pupa, adult) in order with a correct detail about each; Connects the adult laying eggs back to the start of the cycle
- Model: In the Butterfly House, a female monarch’s body makes egg cells by meiosis, so each egg has half the usual chromosomes, and a male’s sperm does too. When a sperm fertilizes an egg, the zygote has the full number again, and it divides by mitosis into the tiny caterpillar growing inside the egg. The caterpillar, or larva, hatches, eats its eggshell and then milkweed, and molts as it grows. Then it hangs upside down and molts into a pupa, the chrysalis, where its body is rebuilt. The adult butterfly breaks out, sips nectar, mates, and lays eggs on milkweed, and the cycle starts again.
