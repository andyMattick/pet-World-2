## Photosynthesis in organisms

How plants, algae, and some bacteria use light energy to make sugar from carbon dioxide and water, and where a plant’s mass really comes from.

**Why it matters: The tree that didn’t eat its dirt.** In the 1600s, a scientist named Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a willow sapling that weighed about 5 pounds in a pot holding 200 pounds of dried soil. For five years he gave it only water. The tree grew to about 169 pounds, but when he dried and weighed the soil again, it had lost only about 2 ounces. So where did the tree come from? Van Helmont guessed water. Scientists later found the biggest part of the answer floating all around us: carbon dioxide in the air.

## Food and energy in organisms

Why living things need food, and how cellular respiration in the mitochondria releases the energy stored in sugar.

**Why it matters: The astronauts who almost ran out of air.** In April 1970, an explosion damaged the Apollo 13 spacecraft on its way to the Moon. The three astronauts moved into the small lunar module, but their own cellular respiration was a problem: every breath out added carbon dioxide, and the lunar module’s air filters were made for two people for two days. The spare filters from the main ship were square, and the holes were round. Engineers on the ground invented a fix from plastic bags, cardboard, a sock and duct tape, the astronauts built it, and all three came home safely.

## Unit test paragraph

You are the head tour guide at the Pet World Greenhouse. Write a paragraph that follows energy and matter from the sunlight on a flower to the wings of a hummingbird that drinks its nectar. Use photosynthesis and cellular respiration, and name what goes in and what comes out of each. (50+ words)

Rubric: (1) Explains photosynthesis: light energy, carbon dioxide, and water make sugar and oxygen in the chloroplasts (2) Explains that the sugar (in the nectar) carries the stored energy to the hummingbird (3) Explains cellular respiration: sugar and oxygen give off carbon dioxide and water and release energy in the mitochondria (4) Says the atoms are rearranged, not destroyed (or that the carbon dioxide can go back to plants)

Model: *It all starts with sunlight. In the flower’s chloroplasts, light energy turns carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil into sugar, and oxygen goes out into the greenhouse. The flower stores some of that sugar in its nectar. When the hummingbird drinks the nectar, the sugar travels in its blood to its wing muscles. There, the mitochondria use oxygen to break the sugar down, which releases the energy to flap its wings and gives off carbon dioxide and water. The atoms are only rearranged, so the carbon dioxide the bird breathes out can be used by the flower again.*

## Review flashcards (`TERM_CARDS.bio3`)

- **photosynthesis**: How plants, algae, and some bacteria use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar, giving off oxygen. *A tomato leaf in the greenhouse making sugar in the sunshine*
- **chloroplast**: The part of a plant or algae cell where photosynthesis happens. *Leaf cells are packed with green chloroplasts.*
- **chlorophyll**: The green substance in chloroplasts that captures light energy. *Chlorophyll is why leaves look green.*
- **glucose**: A sugar made in photosynthesis. Its atoms are carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, and it stores energy. *Plants build starch and wood out of glucose.*
- **carbon dioxide**: A gas in the air. Plants take it in for photosynthesis; cells give it off in cellular respiration. *You breathe out carbon dioxide.*
- **producer**: A living thing that makes its own food from simple materials, usually by photosynthesis. *grass, trees, algae*
- **stomata**: Tiny openings, mostly on the underside of leaves, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapor out. *A leaf’s stomata open in the daytime.*
- **matter**: Anything that has mass and takes up space. In living things its atoms are rearranged, never made or destroyed. *The carbon in a tree’s wood came from carbon dioxide in the air.*
- **variable**: Something that can change in an experiment. Change one on purpose and keep the others the same. *Moving the lamp closer changes the amount of light.*
- **cellular respiration**: How cells use oxygen to break down sugar, releasing energy and giving off carbon dioxide and water. *Your muscle cells doing respiration while you run*
- **mitochondria**: The parts of a cell where cellular respiration releases energy from sugar. Plant and animal cells both have them. *Muscle cells have lots of mitochondria.*
- **energy**: What living things need to move, grow, and stay warm. It is stored in food and released by respiration. *A hummingbird uses a lot of energy to hover.*
- **digestion**: Breaking food into small molecules that the blood can carry to cells. *Pasta is digested into sugars.*
- **breathing**: Moving air into and out of the lungs. It brings in the oxygen for cellular respiration and gets rid of carbon dioxide. *You breathe faster after a sprint.*
- **breathing rate**: How many breaths you take in one minute. It goes up when your cells need more oxygen. *16 breaths a minute resting, 32 after jumping jacks*
- **heart rate**: How many times your heart beats in one minute. A faster heart moves oxygen and sugar to cells faster. *Count your pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4.*
- **fair test**: An experiment that changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same. *Same person, same counting time, before and after exercise*

## Judgment calls to check

- Van Helmont: most of a tree’s **dry** matter comes from carbon dioxide (water adds hydrogen and much of a living tree’s fresh weight). The questions ask what the result shows (not mainly from soil) and what dry matter is built from, so both stay true.
- “Where does the fat go?”: about 84% leaves as exhaled carbon dioxide and the rest as water (Meerman and Brown, BMJ, 2014). The question asks where *most* of it goes.
- Cellular respiration is taught as using oxygen (as Khan’s middle school lesson does). The yeast balloon is called “releasing energy from sugar” without naming fermentation.
